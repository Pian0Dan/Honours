Methods: panel-level association evaluation

Panel generation and representation

Having established how the two-variable populations, survey samples and respondent pools are constructed (?@sec-methodology), the final stage of the pipeline draws an actual panel from the respondent pool. This was done using Panelot, a sortition tool that selects panels of a fixed size \(k\) (here \(k = 50\)) subject to quota constraints on each variable’s margin independently – a minimum and maximum permitted count for every age bracket and every tenure category, specified in a categories.csv file. Rather than returning a single fixed panel, Panelot’s output, lottery.csv, enumerates every feasible panel its selection algorithm discovered, together with the exact probability \(q_j\) with which panel \(j\) would be returned were the lottery run, so that \(\sum_j q_j = 1\) across the \(J\) discovered panels. Each row lists the pool identifiers of the \(k = 50\) selected respondents.

A deliberate feature of this quota structure is that it constrains the age and tenure margins of a panel separately, with no quota placed on their joint (intersectional) distribution. Two panels can therefore satisfy identical quotas – the same count of 18-34 year olds, the same count of renters – while differing substantially in which individuals combine which age with which tenure. This asymmetry between marginal and joint representativeness motivates the association-level evaluation developed below.

Four lottery files were generated in this way, covering every combination of the two populations (independent and associated) and the two response-rate cases (equal-chance and age-associated) introduced previously: lottery_ind_eq.csv, lottery_ind_ass.csv, lottery_cov_eq.csv and lottery_cov_ass.csv.

Linking panels to the survey sample

Each panel’s members are identified only by pool identifier in lottery.csv, so their age and tenure values were recovered by joining back onto the respondent-pool CSV originally supplied to Panelot (the same file written by csv_helper.R, e.g. sim_data2_cov_responded1.csv). This respondent pool – the people who were invited to respond and actually did so – is referred to throughout as the survey sample, and serves as the benchmark against which every panel’s composition is compared.

Within-panel association test

For each discovered panel \(j\), a \(5 \times 3\) contingency table of age against tenure was constructed from its \(k = 50\) members, and a chi-squared test of independence applied to assess whether age and tenure appear related within that panel alone. With only 50 observations spread across 15 cells, several expected cell counts fall below the conventional rule-of-thumb minimum of five, so a Monte Carlo approximation to the null distribution (simulate.p.value = TRUE, \(B = 2000\) resamples) was used in place of the standard asymptotic chi-squared reference distribution.

Panel-versus-sample homogeneity test

The within-panel test alone cannot establish whether a panel’s association differs from the survey sample’s – a panel could show a significant association purely because the sample itself does. To test this directly, each panel \(j\) was compared against the remainder of the survey sample via a three-way log-linear model. Writing \(G\) for a binary group indicator (panel member vs. not), a Poisson model was fitted to the cell counts of the \(5 \times 3 \times 2\) Age \(\times\) Tenure \(\times\) \(G\) table, first without the three-way interaction,

\[ \log \mathbb{E}[\text{Freq}] = \beta_0 + \beta_A^{(a)} + \beta_T^{(t)} + \beta_G^{(g)} + \beta_{AT}^{(a,t)} + \beta_{AG}^{(a,g)} + \beta_{TG}^{(t,g)}, \]

and then with it,

\[ \log \mathbb{E}[\text{Freq}] = \beta_0 + \beta_A^{(a)} + \beta_T^{(t)} + \beta_G^{(g)} + \beta_{AT}^{(a,t)} + \beta_{AG}^{(a,g)} + \beta_{TG}^{(t,g)} + \beta_{ATG}^{(a,t,g)}. \]

A likelihood-ratio test between these two nested models isolates the three-way interaction term \(\beta_{ATG}\): a significant result indicates that the age-tenure association differs between panel members and the rest of the sample, i.e. that panel \(j\)’s intersectional composition is not merely a smaller, noisier version of the sample’s, but systematically different from it.

Multiple testing

Because every panel discovered for a given lottery file is tested this way – several hundred to several thousand panels per file – the homogeneity test was repeated many times over. Results are therefore reported at both an uncorrected threshold of \(\alpha = 0.05\) and a Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted threshold, controlling the false discovery rate across all panels tested within a single lottery file.

Benchmark against random sampling

A panel of only 50 members is itself a small sample, so some disagreement with the full survey sample’s association is expected from sampling variability alone, independent of anything specific to Panelot’s selection mechanism. To separate a genuine effect of quota-constrained selection from this baseline noise, the same homogeneity test was additionally applied to 500 simple random samples of size 50, drawn without any quota constraints from the same survey sample. If panels were no more divergent from the sample than equivalently sized random draws, the proportion of panels flagged as significantly different should resemble the proportion of random samples flagged – approximately \(\alpha\) under the null.

Effect size

Alongside each test, Cramer’s V,

\[ V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2 / n}{\min(r - 1, \, c - 1)}}, \]

was computed from the within-panel contingency table (\(r = 5\) age brackets, \(c = 3\) tenure categories) to express the strength, rather than merely the statistical significance, of the association detected within each panel.

Overview across lottery files

Table 1 summarises the results across all four lottery files. In the associated population, the survey sample itself retains a clearly detectable age-tenure association (Cramer’s V around 0.13), consistent with the negative age-renting relationship built into the population design. In the independent population, by contrast, the sample-level association is negligible and statistically indistinguishable from zero (both \(p > 0.5\)), exactly as expected given that no association was introduced between age and tenure at the population level.

Despite this contrast in the underlying samples, the proportion of panels flagged as holding a significantly different association from their sample is strikingly similar – and strikingly high – across all four files: between 99.8% and 100%, at both the uncorrected and Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted thresholds. In other words, essentially every panel Panelot could possibly return, under either population structure and either response-rate case, exhibits an age-tenure relationship detectably different from the sample it was drawn from – including in the independent population, where there is no true association for a panel to even distort.

Table 2 shows why panel size alone cannot explain the pattern in Table 1. If a 50-member panel were simply a smaller, noisier draw from the survey sample, it should be flagged as significantly different from that sample about as often as an equivalently sized simple random sample is – roughly the nominal \(\alpha\) under the null. Instead, actual sortition panels are flagged essentially every time, and their within-panel Cramer’s V is several times larger than that of a random sample of the same size. The discrepancy therefore cannot be attributed to small-sample variability alone, and instead points to the quota mechanism itself: by fixing the age and tenure margins separately without constraining their joint distribution, Panelot’s selection leaves considerable freedom for which individuals satisfy those margins, and the panels actually discovered exploit that freedom in a way that distorts the joint (intersectional) structure relative to the sample.
